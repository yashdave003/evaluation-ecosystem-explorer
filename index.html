Benchmarks do more than measure.

Once one gains traction, its influence runs in both directions: the same scores that signal progress also reshape how providers allocate research effort, where funders direct capital, and which failure modes regulators attend to. Yet a benchmark is usually judged as a one-shot instrument: calibration, validity, contamination resistance, with the world around it held fixed. This work reframes the question from “is this metric well-calibrated?” to “what does the ecosystem do with this benchmark once it exists?” Our simulation models that ecosystem as six stylized actors (providers, evaluators, consumers, regulators, funders, and media) linked by feedback loops through development, the market, and regulation.

The most common response to benchmark gaming today is to stop showing the test: hold items back, keep the evaluation set private, publish scores less often. That is a decision about the ecosystem, so it is the one we start with.

Does making benchmark content private close the gap between what scores say and what users get?

In the simulation, it helps unevenly. A hidden benchmark is harder to aim at, but what benchmarks reward and what users need still differ, so the gap narrows for some consumer groups and widens for others rather than shrinking overall. This form of holdout design changes where the gap sits more than whether there is one; the explainer's case study walks through the mechanism.

This finding holds under the stylized simulation model presented here, in settings where benchmark and leaderboard results steer what providers build and what buyers, coverage, and capital reward, and where consumer needs are heterogeneous. The simulation generates hypotheses for empirical work to test; the explainer's claims and limitations section states the full boundary, grades each structural assumption by the evidence behind it, and lists what is left out on purpose.

About the simulation

The simulation combines rule-based market dynamics with LLM-driven strategic actors, a form of generative agent-based modeling. Benchmarks, consumer needs, and provider capabilities are all described by weights over six capability dimensions (reasoning, coding, knowledge, safety, communication, agentic). Information is split into tiers: every actor sees public state and holds its own private state (shown when you click its box), while ground truth, meaning true capabilities, true consumer satisfaction, and the benchmarks' true weights, is held by the simulation and hidden from every actor. The gap between what scores say and what users experience is measured against that hidden tier.

Beyond the six actors

The grey boxes make one point: the real ecosystem is much larger, and the six actors do not divide it cleanly. Real organizations sit at intersections: a government safety institute is both an evaluator and an arm of regulation; an arena leaderboard began as academic research and became a venture-backed company; a nonprofit institute funds research grants, co-develops a benchmark, and maintains the harness it runs on. Clicking any box opens its roster; names with a dotted underline play multiple roles, and hovering one traces it across the map.

This ecosystem frame is also distinct from supply-chain and value-chain framings, two neighboring views that deserve their own maps. The AI supply chain (Hopkins et al. 2025) traces how models, datasets, and services compose into products, the chain the EU AI Act regulates as the “AI value chain” (Article 25). And beneath all of it sits compute and infrastructure (semiconductors, data centers, cloud, energy), studied as compute governance (Sastry et al. 2024) and mapped by industry as “the AI value chain.” Neither is modeled in our simulation; both shape what gets built and what gets evaluated.
